# Global Military Spending

Global military spending is the aggregate expenditure by nations on their armed forces and defence capabilities, tracked annually by bodies such as SIPRI and expressed both in absolute terms and as a share of gross domestic product (the "military burden"). It is a structural demand driver for defence manufacturers: rising spending translates into procurement budgets, indigenisation mandates and export opportunities that flow through to suppliers of weapon electronics, subsystems and platforms such as [[entities/apollo_micro_systems_limited]].

## Headline Figures (2025)

According to the source document's review of SIPRI data:

- Global military spending reached **$2,887 billion in 2025**, a 2.9% real-terms increase over 2024.
- The global military burden — military spending as a share of global GDP — held steady at **2.5%**.
- While US spending declined slightly, military expenditure across the rest of the world rose by **9.2%**, showing that growth is no longer driven by a single power.
- Five countries — the United States, China, Russia, Germany and India — accounted for **58% of global military spending**.

## Regional Breakdown

### United States
The US remained the world's largest spender at **$954 billion** in 2025, despite a slight overall decline. As financial military assistance for Ukraine was withdrawn, the country turned inward, investing in conventional and nuclear capabilities under a new National Security Strategy. Military spending approved by Congress for 2026 is expected to surpass **$1 trillion**.

### Europe
Europe was the primary driver of global growth in 2025, with spending rising **14% to $864 billion** — its fastest increase since 1953. The continent is pursuing defensive self-reliance amid US pressure over NATO burden-sharing, and the EU's **ReArm Europe Plan** directs up to **€800 billion** into the sector. Flagship projects targeted for completion by 2028 include the European Drone Defence Initiative, Eastern Flank Watch, European Air Shield and European Space Shield. NATO members are pushing budgets toward or beyond the 2%-of-GDP target. Within the region, Eastern Europe spent $283.9 billion and Central and Western Europe $579.8 billion.

### Asia, Oceania and the Middle East
Asia and Oceania spent **$681 billion**, up 8.1% — the region's largest increase since 2009 — driven by Australia, Japan and the Philippines scaling budgets amid concerns over China's military capabilities and uncertainty about future US support. Sub-regional figures include East Asia $463.9 billion, South Asia $109.9 billion and South East Asia $67.1 billion. Middle Eastern spending reached **$218 billion**, a marginal 0.1% increase, as most regional powers expanded budgets amid elevated tensions.

### Other Regions
- North America: $991.9 billion; Americas total $1,065.3 billion.
- Africa: $58.2 billion (North Africa $35.0 billion; sub-Saharan Africa $23.3 billion).
- South America: $56.3 billion.
- Central Asia: $2.2 billion.

## Growth Drivers

1. **Intensifying military conflicts** — ongoing regional wars, particularly Russia-Ukraine, and tensions in the Middle East and North Africa have driven spending on combat capability and ammunition stocks. The number of interstate armed conflicts doubled from three in 2024 to six in 2025.
2. **European rearmament and NATO commitments** — the ReArm Europe Plan and higher GDP-based targets have produced the fastest European growth since the Cold War.
3. **Technological escalation** — heavy investment in [[concepts/directed-energy-weapons]], artificial intelligence, unmanned platforms and drones, cyber warfare and space-based defence. Unmanned systems in particular are reshaping demand, linking to [[concepts/counter-drone-systems]] and [[concepts/underwater-warfare]].
4. **Defence self-reliance** — nations are shifting away from concentrated international supply chains toward domestic defence-industrial bases to secure supply, exemplified by India's [[concepts/defense_indigenization]] and Make in India push.

## The Supply-Side Response

The spending surge collides with a supply base that is narrow in several critical technologies. Ammunition and energetics restocking has become a bottleneck globally, elevating the strategic value of high-explosives capacity — a market [[entities/idl_explosives]] participates in through bulk, packaged and cartridge explosives, and which underpins the broader [[concepts/defence-energetics]] theme. Similarly, qualified vendors in underwater weapon electronics remain scarce worldwide, which is why torpedo homing capability confers a durable competitive advantage on its holders.

Governments are also rewriting the rules of who may build what. Licences to manufacture weapon systems, such as the lifetime arms manufacturing licence described under [[concepts/defence-manufacturing-licensing]], determine which private firms can capture rising budgets. Transfer of technology from state laboratories, discussed under [[concepts/transfer-of-technology]], converts public research spending into private production capacity.

## Implications for India and for Apollo

India's military burden stood at approximately **2.3% of GDP**, with spending cited at **$92.1 billion** — placing it among the top five global spenders. However, India's response to rising global spending is distinctive: it is channelling a growing share of procurement to domestic industry. The Union Budget for FY 2026-27 allocated roughly **₹7.8–7.85 lakh crore** to the Ministry of Defence, of which about **75% of the capital acquisition budget** is reserved for domestic firms.

This creates a dual opportunity captured in the source document's framing of [[concepts/tier_1_defense_oem_integration]]:

- **Domestic deepening** — Indian defence production reached ~₹1.78 lakh crore with 15.6% YoY growth, and near-65% of equipment is now produced domestically.
- **Export ambition** — Indian defence exports hit a record **₹38,424 crore** in FY2025-26, up 62.66%, to over 80 countries. See [[concepts/defense-exports-and-global-market-entry]].

The global environment also shifts the calculus for trusted supply-chain partners. Fragmentation has accelerated the reorientation of defence supply chains away from a small number of supplier nations, giving countries with competitive costs and neutral geopolitical posture an opening as alternative sources of platforms, subsystems and components.

## Strategic Takeaways

- Global military spending is in a sustained upcycle, but growth has broadened beyond the United States to Europe, Asia and the Middle East.
- The composition of spending is shifting toward munitions restocking, autonomous systems, electronic warfare and layered air-defence/counter-drone capabilities.
- Rising budgets are increasingly paired with industrial-policy strings — indigenisation lists, local-content thresholds and licensed manufacturing — that reshape competitive dynamics.
- For firms positioned across electronics, energetics and platform integration, the combination of higher budgets and supply-chain diversification is a multi-year demand tailwind.
